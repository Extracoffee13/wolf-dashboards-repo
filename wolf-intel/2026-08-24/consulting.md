## 1. BCG — "Enterprise AI Control Plane: The CIO's Guide to Governing and Accelerating AI Agents"

**Firm:** BCG
**Source:** https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/how-cios-govern-ai-agents-at-scale
**Published:** ~2026-08-10

**Summary:** BCG argues that AI agent deployment is outrunning enterprise governance, and that fragmented, platform-by-platform control (one policy per tool, per business unit) is now the binding constraint on scaling agentic AI rather than model capability. Its proposed fix is an "Enterprise AI Control Plane" — a unifying layer for identity, policy enforcement, visibility, and audit across every agent, regardless of which platform spawned it. The pitch: organizations that build standardized "golden paths" with governance baked in, rather than bolted on, deploy agents faster *and* with less risk, because governance stops being a per-project tax and becomes shared infrastructure.

**What it means for The Construct:** This is the exact shape of problem Hartley Capital's agent-AI-ops thesis needs solved before it can be sold to a PE roll-up or family office as infrastructure, not a science project — a fragmented per-tool governance model is a liability story that kills a pitch to a risk-averse allocator, so any Construct-adjacent agent-ops offering should lead with a control-plane narrative, not a capability-count narrative.

**Score: 4** — sharpens the Hartley Capital agent-AI-ops thesis; doesn't change it, but gives it the governance vocabulary institutional buyers will expect.

## 2. BCG — "AI-First Enterprise Operations: Reinventing the Operating System of Work"

**Firm:** BCG
**Source:** https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/reinventing-the-operating-system-of-work-with-ai
**Published:** ~2026-07-07

**Summary:** BCG's core claim is that most enterprises are getting task-level AI lift (faster individual steps) without structural lift, because they're layering agents onto unchanged processes instead of redesigning the process around agent-driven control flow. The firms actually capturing outsized value — up to 80% straight-through processing, 60%+ cost-out, 30% CLTV uplift in early cases — are the ones that rebuilt the operating system of work itself: outcome-managed agents with orchestration logic replacing human hand-offs, not agents bolted onto the old flowchart.

**What it means for The Construct: ** Confirms the economics only show up at the "rebuild the operating model" altitude, not the "add a chatbot to step 3" altitude — which is the argument for going deep on one or two operations (PE roll-up back-office, hedge fund ops) rather than spreading agent tooling thin across many surface-level use cases.

**Score: 4** — sharpens the same thesis from the delivery-economics side: the 60%+ cost-out number is a concrete, quotable benchmark for pitching agent-ops engagements.

## 3. arXiv — "Mind Viruses: Self-Propagating Ideas in Multi-Agent LLM Systems" (2608.10218)

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10218
**Published:** 2026-08-10

**Summary:** The paper formalizes "mind viruses" — ideas or goals that propagate through a multi-agent LLM system by inducing the agents that adopt them to transmit them onward to other agents, independent of whether the idea is true or useful. It's a safety/dynamics paper, not a product paper: the contribution is a framework for detecting and measuring this kind of unwanted belief propagation in systems where many agents talk to each other and update on each other's outputs.

**What it means for The Construct:** A durable risk to keep on the list for any multi-agent build (PRAXIS, the WOLF Committee's four-role debate structure, Hermes' profile-switching) — the more agents cross-reference each other's outputs as ground truth, the more a single bad premise can propagate uncorrected through the whole system before a human notices; worth a periodic "does the Committee actually disagree, or is it echoing" audit.

**Score: 3** — useful background, not thesis-moving; flag for the multi-agent-systems watch list rather than act on immediately.
