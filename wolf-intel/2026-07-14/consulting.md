## 1. Family offices adjust asset allocation to meet evolving challenges

**Firm:** Roland Berger
**Source:** https://www.rolandberger.com/en/Insights/Publications/Family-offices-adjust-asset-allocation-to-meet-evolving-challenges.html
**Date:** July 2026 (exact day within the window not independently confirmed on-page; flagged moderate confidence)

**Summary:** Roland Berger surveyed 88 DACH-region family offices on asset allocation and risk perception. The headline shift: geopolitical upheaval has overtaken interest-rate uncertainty as the top perceived risk (88% citing it, vs. 65% a year prior) — a reversal from a macro-rate-driven risk framework to a geopolitical one. Capital is rotating accordingly: private equity and defensive assets (precious metals) show the strongest allocation momentum, while sector priorities cluster around infrastructure, healthcare, and AI. The report reads as a signal that family-office capital, historically slow-moving and conservative, is repricing tail risk faster than public markets are.

**What it means for The Construct:** This is Hartley Capital's exact LP profile validating its own playbook in real time — family offices are moving toward PE structures and away from pure rate-sensitivity at the same moment Hartley is positioning agent-run roll-ups as a defensive-plus-AI-upside vehicle. The geopolitical framing also strengthens the case for domestically-anchored, cash-flowing real assets (FL real estate, monument signage, homebuilder-adjacent services) over globally-exposed growth bets — exactly the Brand 9 engine's positioning, whether or not that was the intent.

**Score: 5** — sharpens (arguably confirms) a thesis we hold about where family-office capital is headed and why Hartley Capital's structure is well-timed.

## 2. Managed Autonomy at Runtime: Gear-Based Safety and Governance for Single- and Multi-Agent Cyber-Physical Systems

**Source:** arXiv:2607.00334 (cs.AI, week of 2026-07-07)
**Authors:** Srini Ramaswamy, Wang Miaosheng
**URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00334
**Date confidence:** moderate — arXiv ID numbering places it early July 2026; exact day within the 7–14 window not independently confirmed.

**Summary:** Proposes a "gear-based" runtime governance model for autonomous single- and multi-agent cyber-physical systems — discrete autonomy levels ("gears") that shift dynamically based on risk state, with formal safety guarantees at each transition. It's aimed at systems where agents operate with real consequences and need supervised, auditable throttling of independence rather than a binary on/off autonomy switch.

**What it means for The Construct:** This is close to the governance problem WOLF and the broader PRAXIS pipeline haven't formally solved — right now agent autonomy is implicit (what each agent is allowed to do is scattered across prompts and permissions) rather than a first-class, auditable "gear" that shifts with risk. As The Construct scales agent count (WOLF, PRAXIS-fed agents, Hartley Capital's operational agents), a formal autonomy-gear model is a plausible blueprint for how the fleet self-throttles exposure without a human in every loop — worth a real design read, not just a citation.

**Score: 4** — sharpens the agent-ops-at-scale thesis directly; this is infrastructure The Construct will eventually need to build or adopt.

## 3. The Industrial AI Divide: How AI Leaders Are Pulling Ahead Across Transportation, Logistics, and Defense

**Firm:** Oliver Wyman Forum (with UC Berkeley)
**Source:** https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights.html (specific article permalink not independently fetchable due to site block; landing page and title confirmed via search)
**Date:** July 14, 2026 (explicit in search result)

**Summary:** Based on 50+ executive interviews and Oliver Wyman Forum survey data across transportation, logistics, and defense. Only 8% of CEOs in these sectors report using AI at scale — yet the early movers who do are seeing AI-enabled revenue gains above 20%. The report argues AI strategy has to move out of IT and into the C-suite/boardroom, because the gap between AI-native and AI-laggard operators is widening faster than typical technology-adoption curves would predict.

**What it means for The Construct:** This is the PE roll-up thesis rendered as a single data point — an 8%-adopted, 20%-upside gap means the laggard 92% of operators in these unglamorous, capital-intensive sectors are underpriced acquisition targets for anyone who can bolt on agent-AI operations post-close, which is precisely the Hartley Capital playbook. The open question the report doesn't answer is the compression window: if the gap is closing in 18–24 months rather than the multi-year horizon consultants usually assume, the roll-up arbitrage window is shorter than the fund's current planning horizon implies.

**Score: 4** — sharpens the AI-adoption-arbitrage thesis underneath PE roll-ups; useful, not thesis-changing on its own.
