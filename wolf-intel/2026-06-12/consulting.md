### Executive Summary

BCG argues that real estate is structurally overexposed to exactly the class of inefficiency that AI is mature enough to address at scale — yet the sector has not acted. Two data points anchor the thesis: 66% of real estate projects experience schedule overruns, and 39% face cost overruns, both driven by fragmentation and rework. Against this backdrop, only 25% of real estate firms qualify as AI leaders, versus 40% across all industries, and the sector invests roughly half the cross-industry average in AI. The window for first-mover advantage is described as "narrow."

For development companies specifically (DevCos), BCG quantifies the stakes precisely: AI-first implementations deliver 400–700 basis points of margin uplift through applications including construction site AI assistants, procurement optimization, and automated subcontractor coordination, while compressing project timelines by approximately 30%. The prescribed transformation playbook requires CEO ownership of the transformation (not CTO delegation), a single BIM-centric data model as the foundation, an AI delivery office for governance and cross-functional coordination, and multiyear targets tied directly to asset value creation rather than pilot metrics.

BCG's framing explicitly positions this as an early-mover race: the competitive gap between AI leaders and laggards in real estate will widen faster than in most sectors because the sector's fragmented ownership structures create durable data moats once established.

### Executive Summary

EY and the University of Cambridge argue that the global infrastructure sector's $64 trillion funding gap (against $140T needed by 2050) is not purely a capital problem — it is a coordination failure addressable by agentic AI deployed as a connective intelligence layer. Rework alone accounts for up to 15% of total project costs, with hundreds of billions of dollars in annual value destroyed not by technical failures but by fragmented systems that cannot communicate with each other. Infrastructure productivity has flatlined despite billions in digital tooling investment: each new sensor and platform adds data to an environment already saturated with it, producing noise rather than decision support.

The report's core proposition is architectural: agentic AI does not replace infrastructure tools, it orchestrates them. An agentic layer sits above existing systems — BIM platforms, procurement software, scheduling tools, IoT sensor networks — and synthesizes their outputs into real-time actionable decisions, enabling infrastructure professionals to shift from administrative coordination to high-judgment work. The governance framework specifies five co-equal pillars required for production deployment: accountability and legal liability; transparency and explainability; data sovereignty and commercial confidentiality; human oversight and competence; and systemic resilience.

The collaboration with NVIDIA and Futurity Systems suggests this is not purely theoretical — the report is oriented toward near-term deployability in large-scale infrastructure programs.

This is the conceptual architecture for Hartley Capital's agent-ops infrastructure build — the "intelligence layer above fragmented systems" framing should directly inform how Hartley designs AI orchestration across portfolio companies, and the five-pillar governance framework is a ready-made due diligence checklist for AI-enabled portco acquisitions.
