EY has published the most granular public accounting of what agentic AI actually costs an enterprise to run at scale. The headline: a simple 2023-era linear AI workflow (input → retrieval → response) cost $0.04/interaction. A 2026 orchestrated agentic system — tools, reasoning loops, multi-step decisions — costs $1.20/interaction, a 30x increase. But token costs are only one dimension. EY maps the full cost stack: infrastructure, knowledge-base maintenance, governance overhead, organizational change management, failure recovery, and regulatory risk. The key strategic insight is that agentic AI has fundamentally shifted enterprise AI from a fixed-cost labor displacement model to a dynamic compute consumption model — more like cloud infrastructure than headcount reduction. Boards and CFOs are now demanding investment cases that capture the total operating picture, including Agent FinOps as a managed function. EY proposes five governance pillars for agentic deployments: accountability/legal liability; transparency/explainability; data sovereignty; human oversight competence; and systemic resilience.

**What it means for The Construct:**  
Anyone at Hartley Capital modeling AI agent deployment into fund operations or portfolio companies has been using 2023-era cost assumptions. The 30x cost jump for orchestrated agents means the ROI model needs rebuilding from scratch — the financial case only works at scale if the task being automated has high enough unit value. Agent FinOps is now a real function that needs a dedicated budget line and a cost-per-decision metric, not a per-seat license analogy.

### 3. BCG — "When Everyone Uses AI, Companies Risk Losing Critical Skills"
**Score: 4 / 5 — Sharpens a thesis**  
**Firm:** Boston Consulting Group  
**Published:** June 2026  
**URL:** https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/when-everyone-uses-ai-companies-risk-critical-skills

**Executive Summary:**  
BCG's research identifies a structural risk that most AI adoption frameworks ignore: the skills organizations consider most critical to long-term competitive advantage — judgment and decision-making, problem framing, creative thinking — are precisely the skills most at risk from AI-driven deskilling. The mechanism is subtle: AI agents now handle multistep workflows end-to-end (30% of companies, up from 13% a year ago), compressing the cognitive loops that build human capability. The junior-to-senior mentoring chain through which firms transmit tacit knowledge, professional judgment, and craft is being eroded by the same automation that drives efficiency gains. BCG's prescription includes: preserving human ownership of final judgments and accountability for outcomes; designing structured apprenticeships and coaching relationships that survive AI automation; and explicit work-allocation design that specifies which tasks stay human, which go to AI, and where ensemble approaches apply. The report frames this as a long-cycle organizational risk — one that won't manifest in the next earnings call but will show up in degraded decision quality 5–10 years out.

**What it means for The Construct:**  
For Hartley Capital, the alpha edge in PE is judgment — thesis development, pattern recognition across markets, deal evaluation. If the analytical pipeline that trains junior analysts is outsourced to agents, the next generation of partners will have processed more data and developed less judgment. The deskilling risk is the dark side of the agent AI thesis that most PE firms haven't priced in.
