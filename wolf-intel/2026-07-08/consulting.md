## 1. Return on AI: How CFOs and CIOs Can Manage the Token Meter

**Firm:** BCG (BCG X / Henderson Institute lineage) — published **July 1, 2026**
**Source:** https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/managing-ai-token-costs
Related: https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/how-ceos-can-optimize-ai-token-costs

**Summary:** As AI usage scales past pilots into production, token consumption is exploding across functions — and BCG argues traditional FinOps can't keep pace, especially once AI agents (which chain calls, retry, and self-loop) become the dominant consumer. BCG proposes "Return on AI" (RoAI) as the governing metric: a workflow-level operating model where leadership can (1) see what's being spent and on what, (2) shape the cost via routing, caching, model tiering, and prompt discipline, and (3) prove value or kill the workflow. The piece is explicit that tokens now hit the P&L on three separate lines — capex, opex, and COGS — and argues the winners won't be firms with the smallest or largest AI bill, but the ones with the highest return per token spent.

**What it means for The Construct:** This is a mirror, not an abstraction — WOLF, PRAXIS, and the rest of the agent fleet are exactly the "AI agents pushing the meter into overdrive" BCG is describing, and we currently have no workflow-level RoAI accounting layer. We should treat this as a near-term build item: per-routine token attribution (which agent, which task, which output) before spend scales further, not after.

**Score: 5** — changes a thesis we hold (that agent-ops cost governance is a "later" problem). BCG is telling us it's a now problem, and we're already running the pattern they describe.

## 2. Family Office Study: New Asset Allocation

**Firm:** Roland Berger — published **July 2026** (this week)
**Source:** https://www.rolandberger.com/en/Insights/Publications/Family-offices-adjust-asset-allocation-to-meet-evolving-challenges.html

**Summary:** Roland Berger's fresh family office survey (DACH-region primary sample, directionally read as a proxy for global family office behavior) finds a clear rotation: private equity — via funds and, where internal expertise allows, direct deals — is now the strongest-growth allocation, sharing momentum with defensive assets like precious metals. Venture capital and crypto/digital assets are being actively scaled back by a growing share of respondents. By sector, infrastructure is the top thematic priority, followed by healthcare and AI, with AI "gaining ground rapidly." Operationally, cybersecurity has overtaken interest-rate uncertainty as family offices' top concern, followed by digitalization and professionalizing internal structures. Geopolitical upheaval has replaced rate uncertainty as the top external risk.

**What it means for The Construct: ** Family office LPs are moving exactly the direction Hartley Capital needs them to move — out of VC/crypto, into PE (funds and direct) — while simultaneously prioritizing AI and "professionalized internal structures," which is a soft pitch for operators who can show a real AI-native operating model, not just capital access. This is a tailwind for Hartley's fundraising narrative, provided the AI-agent-ops story is concrete and demonstrable, not decorative.

**Score: 4** — sharpens the Hartley Capital LP-sourcing thesis; doesn't change it, but gives us live language ("professionalization," "direct deal capability," "AI as thematic priority") to mirror back to family office allocators.

## 3. Private Equity Midyear Report 2026: Control the Controllable, Weather the Rest

**Firm:** Bain & Company — published **June 8, 2026** (outside strict 24-48h window; included for materiality — see scan notes)
**Source:** https://www.bain.com/insights/private-equity-midyear-report-2026/

**Summary:** Bain documents a stalled PE recovery: three compounding shocks — an AI-driven "SaaSpocalypse" in software valuations, redemption stress in private credit, and an oil-price spike from the Iran conflict — knocked deal, fundraising, and exit activity back down in H1 2026. Tech deal value fell 70% quarter-over-quarter into Q1. The headline structural finding is Bain's "12 is the new 5" framework: a decade ago, a buyout needed only ~5% annual EBITDA growth to clear a 2.5x return; today it needs roughly 10-12%, because multiple expansion and leverage can no longer be relied on to do the work. Bain's prescription is disciplined, AI-enabled value-creation plans rather than broad-based deal volume.

**What it means for The Construct:** This is the number Hartley Capital's roll-up underwriting needs to re-run against — if our deal models still assume mid-single-digit organic EBITDA growth plus multiple expansion, they're pricing off a market that no longer exists. Combined with item #2 (family office capital rotating hard into PE), the read is: more capital chasing roll-ups, but the bar to actually clear a target return has roughly doubled — which favors roll-ups with a genuine operating-leverage story (agent-driven ops cost reduction) over financially-engineered ones.

**Score: 5** — changes a thesis we hold: the implicit growth assumption underlying roll-up economics needs to be re-underwritten now, not at the next fund close.
