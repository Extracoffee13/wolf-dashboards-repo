### [3] Authorization Propagation in Multi-Agent AI Systems: Identity Governance as Infrastructure
**Source:** arxiv / Kamiwaza AI (preprint)
**Author:** Krti Tallam
**Submitted:** May 6, 2026
**arXiv ID:** 2605.05440
**URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05440
**Score: 4 — sharpens a thesis**

**Executive Summary**

This paper formalizes a problem that the strategy firms are gesturing at but haven't solved: in multi-agent AI systems, classical access-control frameworks (RBAC, ABAC, ReBAC) fail because they assume human principals making discrete, auditable decisions. When non-human agents retrieve data, delegate tasks, and synthesize results autonomously across shifting boundaries, authorization invariants break in ways prompt-injection defenses don't catch. Tallam identifies three sub-problems — transitive delegation (Agent A grants Agent B authority it shouldn't have downstream), aggregation inference (combining individually authorized data reveals unauthorized patterns), and temporal validity (permissions granted at t=0 may be stale or escalated by t=N) — and derives seven structural requirements for authorization architecture in multi-agent systems. The paper frames identity governance not as a security feature bolted on, but as foundational infrastructure without which multi-agent systems at scale are architecturally unsound.

**What It Means for The Construct**

Any multi-agent system Hartley Capital deploys for fund operations — diligence automation, deal sourcing, portfolio monitoring — inherits these three failure modes by default. Building to these seven structural requirements from the start is worth six months of future remediation and the reputational exposure of an authorization failure in a regulated capital context.
