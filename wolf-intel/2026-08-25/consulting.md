## 1. An Economy of AI Agents

**Authors:** Gillian K. Hadfield (Johns Hopkins) & Andrew Koh (MIT)
**Source:** Chapter prepared for the NBER *Handbook on the Economics of Transformative AI*; also posted to arXiv (2509.01063)
**URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.01063 · https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/economics-transformative-ai/economy-ai-agents

**Executive summary:** This is the closest thing yet to a foundational economics treatment of what happens when AI agents — not just AI tools — become independent-ish actors in markets and organizations. Hadfield and Koh survey how agents that plan and execute over long horizons with little human oversight will interact with each other and with humans, what new market failures and coordination problems emerge (agent-to-agent contracting, liability when an agent's counterpart is another agent, trust and reputation systems for non-human principals), and what institutions — legal, contractual, technical — will need to exist for an "agent economy" to function without collapsing into fraud, collusion, or unaccountable cascades of automated decisions. It's explicitly a research agenda, not a product pitch: the value is in naming the open questions precisely.

**What it means for The Construct:** This is the theory-of-the-firm paper for exactly the structure WOLF/PRAXIS is already running live — a household economy of semi-autonomous agents transacting, delegating, and holding each other accountable. The open questions Hadfield/Koh raise (agent-to-agent trust, liability chains, institutional scaffolding) are the ones The Construct will hit in practice before the literature catches up; worth a standing watch on this line of academic work as a check on our own governance gaps.
**Score: 5**

## 2. FINTRX Q2 2026 Family Office Report

**Firm:** FINTRX (family office intelligence/data platform — not a top-10 strategy firm, but directly on-thesis for the Hartley Capital family-office-trends brief and more current than anything the top 10 published this cycle)
**URL:** https://www.fintrx.com/blog/fintrx-q2-2026-family-office-report-shows-firms-favor-bespoke-direct-investments-over-hedge-funds-and-private-credit
**Published:** ~2026-08-19

**Executive summary:** Among the 96 new family offices FINTRX added to its platform in Q2 2026, 92.7% expressed interest in direct investments and 89.6% in private equity — versus just 10.4% for hedge funds and 6.3% for private credit. That's the widest divergence between new-cohort and full-database preference FINTRX has tracked: hedge fund interest across the whole database sits at 38.2% (a −27.8pp gap vs. new entrants) and private credit at 24.1% (−17.9pp). The shift correlates with the composition of who's forming family offices now: 68.6% of Q2's newly classified offices are first-generation, entrepreneur-origin wealth (up from 57% in Q1), sourced disproportionately from private investing, technology, and real estate — i.e., operators who made their money by running something directly and want to keep doing that with their capital, not hand it to a commingled vehicle.

**What it means for The Construct:** This is a live tailwind for Hartley Capital's positioning as an operator-run PE roll-up rather than a fund LPs passively allocate to — the newest, fastest-growing cohort of family-office capital is explicitly rejecting the hedge-fund/private-credit wrapper in favor of direct control. It sharpens (doesn't create) the existing thesis that agent-AI-run operating companies courting family-office co-investment directly, with transparent operating metrics instead of a black-box fund structure, are underpriced relative to where this capital wants to go.
**Score: 4**

## 3. The Agentic Leadership Playbook: The COO as Enterprise Orchestrator

**Firm:** BCG
**URL:** https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/coo-role-becomes-ai-orchestrator
**Published:** 2026-08-12

**Executive summary:** BCG's framing: agentic AI is pushing operations from periodic optimization to continuous orchestration, and the COO role is shifting from technical operator to orchestrator of a standing fleet of AI agents across procurement, logistics, and forecasting. Cited figures: leading deployments improving on-time-in-full 2–3%, shipment logistics 15–20%, inventory 10–25%, with fully orchestrated deployments cutting scrap up to 50%. The piece argues workforce cost savings are the smaller part of the value case — a $10–20M procurement team can influence $5B in external spend, so orchestration-driven decision quality dwarfs headcount efficiency.
**What it means for The Construct:** Useful competitive-signal background on what "state of the art" agent orchestration looks like at F500 scale — a benchmark for how far ahead (or behind) The Construct's own agent-ops stack is versus what big-consulting is now selling into the enterprise. Background, not thesis-moving.
**Score: 3**
