# Spike: Why do CRE developers procure signage late, despite early signage boosting leasing velocity?

**Date:** 2026-05-05  
**Relevant domains:** Brand 9 Signs, Construct, Hartley Capital, signage industry primitives, CRE fundamentals  
**Delta category:** novel

---

## Question

Why do commercial real estate developers procure signage late in the project timeline, even though visible signage (especially "NOW LEASING" and monument identification) demonstrably affects leasing velocity from an earlier point in the asset lifecycle?

---

## First-Principles Answer

### Starting primitives

A sign is a spatially-fixed broadcast medium. Its economic value equals (impressions × conversion rate × lease value per conversion). Impressions are determined by location traffic, not ad spend. This makes signage, at its root, a leasing marketing tool with a fixed cost basis — not a construction finish item.

### Tracing the procurement delay from first principles

**1. Information dependency (real but overstated)**  
Tenant-listing signs (monument panels, suite directories) require knowing tenant names. Lease execution is often 60-120 days before occupancy. This creates a genuine upstream dependency for tenant-ID content. However, developers conflate *tenant-ID signage* with *availability signage*. "NOW LEASING — 12,000 SF Available" requires zero tenant information and could be installed at permit. The conflation delays both categories unnecessarily.

**2. Pro forma blindspot**  
Standard CRE pro formas model vacancy as a flat absorption assumption (e.g., "stabilization at month 18"). They do not model: *cost of sign vs. days of vacancy eliminated by sign*. A monument sign at $18,000 that pulls in one 5,000 SF tenant 30 days earlier recovers its cost in under two weeks at typical NNN rents. This ROI calculation is never run because signage is a construction line item, categorically separated from leasing economics. The two budgets don't talk; neither team owns the full equation.

**3. Organizational siloing**  
The construction/project management team controls signage procurement. Their incentive ends at certificate of occupancy — on time, on budget. Leasing velocity is the asset manager's or broker's problem. The leasing team would benefit from earlier signage but holds no procurement authority. This is a classic principal-agent mismatch: the party that bears the cost of late signage (extended vacancy) is not the party who controls the timing decision.

**4. Risk asymmetry in custom fabrication**  
Custom monument signs have 8-14 week lead times. If ordered at permit (18-24 months before TCO on large projects), they risk arriving mid-construction with no clean installation window. This is a real logistics risk that encourages deferral. However, the solution is not "order late" — it is structural pre-pour at permit, panel fabrication at 90-day TCO. The industry default collapses both decisions into one deferred action.

**5. Modular decoupling as the structural fix**  
The solution derivable from first principles: separate the installation-structure decision (electrical stub, footing, mounting system) from the content decision (panels, tenant listings). Install structure early, swap panels as tenant roster is confirmed. This decouples the "order early" from "need final tenant list" problem. It requires a sign company that designs for modularity and can serve as a long-term partner rather than a one-time fabricator.

### Conclusion

Late signage procurement is not irrational given the incentive structure developers operate in — it is the predictable output of (a) information dependency conflating two different sign types, (b) pro forma structure that severs the cost of signage from the leasing benefit it creates, (c) organizational siloing between construction and leasing, and (d) custom fabrication logistics. The opportunity for Brand 9 Signs is to reframe signage as a *leasing velocity instrument* and offer modular systems that let developers act early without needing complete information.

## Corpus Answer

Industry sources (Flexlume, National Signs, South Coast Improvement) confirm that late signage procurement is common and treat it as a **construction coordination failure**: permitting complexity, change orders, electrical coordination, and landlord-approval sequencing create friction that teams resolve by deferring the decision.

The practitioner recommendation is to bring sign partners in during pre-construction — framed as protecting the construction schedule, reducing change orders, and avoiding reopened finished surfaces.

**What the corpus does not say:**  
- No source connects signage timing to leasing velocity  
- No source models signage as a leasing marketing tool with calculable ROI against vacancy cost  
- No source identifies the pro forma blindspot or the org-chart principal-agent mismatch as root causes  
- No source proposes modular structure/panel decoupling as the architectural fix

---

## Delta: novel

My first-principles derivation and the corpus agree on the observable phenomenon (late procurement is the industry norm). The corpus correctly identifies coordination friction as a proximate cause. However, my derivation surfaces the deeper causal layer: **pro forma structure and organizational siloing are the root causes, not coordination complexity**. The corpus treats late procurement as a construction problem; the first-principles lens identifies it as a leasing economics problem.

The "signage as leasing velocity instrument" framing, the ROI calculation against vacancy cost, and the structural fix (modular early-install decoupling) are absent from available corpus sources.
