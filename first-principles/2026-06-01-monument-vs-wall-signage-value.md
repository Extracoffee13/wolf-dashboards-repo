# Spike: Monument vs. Wall Signage — What Drives the Value Premium?

**Date:** 2026-06-01  
**Category:** novel  
**Confidence:** 0.72

---

## Question

What is the first-order economic determinant of monument vs. wall-mounted signage value in commercial real estate — and does the standard industry answer explain the mechanism correctly?

---

## First-Principles Answer

### What does signage fundamentally do?

Signage creates **recognition events** in observers capable of acting on that recognition. Economic value is:

```
Value = (recognition events / time) × P(conversion | recognition) × (margin per conversion)
        minus (fabrication + installation + permitting + maintenance)
```

The question reduces to: why do monument signs generate more or higher-quality recognition events than wall signs?

### Viewing geometry

**Wall sign:** Readable primarily along an axis perpendicular to the building face. Dwell window is short for vehicular traffic. Typically elevated — the observer must look up and away from travel path.

**Monument sign:** Positioned at grade near a property ingress. Three things converge at that point:

1. **Dwell-time spike** — vehicles decelerate to turn; pedestrians pause at curbs. The observer slows exactly at the sign.
2. **Eye-level alignment** — the sign sits in the same focal plane as the road ahead, not competing with upward gaze.
3. **Decision-moment alignment** — the observer is at the binary routing moment: *enter now, or continue past.*

The monument sign intercepts traffic *at the routing decision*, not before it (brand awareness) or after it (reinforcement). This is the mechanism the corpus mostly misses.

### Scarcity analysis

Any property has one or two ingress points regardless of how many tenants share the building. Monument sign positions are therefore O(1) per property while wall positions are O(n) by surface area. The landlord holds a monopoly on a non-reproducible viewing geometry. Scarcity is structural, not artificial.

### Pricing derivation

The landlord can extract rent up to — but not exceeding — the incremental revenue the tenant earns from superior traffic capture. The premium is bounded by:

```
(extra captured turns / day) × (conversion rate) × (margin per customer) × (lease days)
```

Fabrication cost (foundation, electrical, heavier materials) is a **floor**, not a ceiling, on pricing. The ceiling is economic capture value from decision-moment intercept.

### Prediction before corpus search

The corpus will cite prestige, permanence, and fabrication cost. It will assert "greater visibility" as a brute fact without deriving the mechanism from traffic geometry or routing decisions.

## Corpus Answer

Sources consulted:
- Duval Sign Solutions — monument signs guide
- Hughes Marino — negotiating commercial signage
- Hollander Real Estate Law — signage provisions in leases
- Ideal Print — commercial real estate sign budgeting

**What the corpus says:**
- Monument signs cost more to build: $150–$400/sq ft, plus $500–2,500 for structural engineering, $300–1,500 electrical
- Premium justified by: "curb appeal," "permanence," "looks like it's here to stay," "perceived quality and professionalism"
- ROI language: "increased traffic," "faster lease-ups," "higher community recognition"
- Lease law: monument rights are distinct from building signage rights; often reserved for anchor tenants or negotiated separately

**What the corpus does not say:**
- No articulation of decision-moment geometry
- No analysis of dwell-time spike at ingress points
- No derivation of scarcity from O(1) ingress count
- Distinction between brand awareness (wall sign) and routing capture (monument sign) is absent

## Delta Analysis

| Dimension | Corpus | First Principles |
|---|---|---|
| Why premium exists | Prestige + fabrication cost | Decision-moment capture + positional scarcity |
| Visibility mechanism | Asserted ("greater visibility") | Derived (dwell-time spike + eye-level alignment at routing binary) |
| Pricing floor | Fabrication cost | Confirmed |
| Pricing ceiling | Not addressed | Incremental captured turns × conversion × margin |
| Scarcity source | Not analyzed | O(1) ingress positions per property |

**Category: `novel`**

The corpus is not wrong — prestige and fabrication cost are real factors. But it explains the symptom without the mechanism. Decision-moment geometry and structural positional scarcity are the actual load-bearing reasons the asset commands a premium. These frames don't appear in the standard industry literature surveyed.

## Commentary

This matters for Brand 9 Signs operationally: when advising commercial clients, the pitch for monument signage can be grounded in traffic-capture economics rather than brand prestige alone. The prestige argument is soft; the routing-decision argument is quantifiable (traffic counts × turn rates × conversion estimates). That shifts the conversation from aesthetics to ROI, which is more durable in a B2B sales context.

It also matters for Hartley Capital real estate evaluation: monument sign rights on a commercial property are a genuine scarce asset, not just an amenity. They should be evaluated as such in lease negotiations and property underwriting.
