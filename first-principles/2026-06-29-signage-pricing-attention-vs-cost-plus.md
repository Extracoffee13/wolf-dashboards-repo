# First-Principles Spike: Signage Pricing — Attention-Value vs. Cost-Plus

**Date:** 2026-06-29
**Question:** Why does signage pricing default to substrate + labor rather than attention-value delivered, and what would first-principles pricing look like?
**Delta Category:** novel
**Confidence:** 0.72

---

## First-Principles Answer

### Starting from the irreducible function of a sign

A sign's economic job is singular: transmit information from a sender to a viewer at a fixed location, and convert that viewer's attention into behavior — a purchase, a turn, a brand impression, a decision.

That function means the unit of value a sign creates is:

> **attention-impressions × message-conversion-rate × purchase-value-per-conversion**

Not square footage of substrate. Not hours of vinyl application. Those are inputs, not outputs.

### Why cost-plus pricing dominates anyway

Working forward from the incentive structure of a sign shop transaction:

1. **Information asymmetry.** The shop can measure its substrate cost and labor hours precisely. It cannot measure how many eyeballs a finished sign will capture, or what revenue lift the buyer will see. Without a measurable output unit, pricing defaults to the measurable input unit.

2. **One-shot transaction model.** A sign shop typically sells once and installs. There is no ongoing relationship through which the shop could capture a share of value-over-time (the way a landlord captures rent, or an ad platform captures CPM). Without tenure in the value stream, the shop settles for a one-time cost-recovery + margin payment.

3. **Buyer-side commoditization.** Buyers RFQ sign shops with a spec sheet: dimensions, substrate, quantity. This creates apples-to-apples price comparison on visible inputs, which collapses differentiation and drives shops toward cost-based quotes to win bids. The buyer's procurement behavior imposes cost-plus on the seller.

4. **Risk allocation.** The shop bears fabrication risk (materials wasted, labor hours lost). The buyer bears business-performance risk (will this sign bring customers?). The pricing model matches the risk allocation: the shop charges for what it controls, not for outcomes it can't guarantee.

5. **Attribution opacity.** Even if a sign does drive $40,000 in incremental annual sales, the retailer cannot cleanly attribute that to the sign vs. foot traffic growth vs. a competitor closing. With attribution unclear, neither party can negotiate around a value number.

### What first-principles pricing would look like

If value is attention × conversion × purchase-value, then pricing should anchor to **attention delivered** — the same primitive the advertising and media industry uses:

- **CPM-style pricing by location traffic.** A monument sign on a 40,000-cars-per-day arterial delivers ~14 million impressions/year. A billboard advertiser would pay $2,000–8,000/month for that reach. A sign shop building a permanent monument earns $3,000 once. The gap is the captured-value opportunity.

- **Traffic-tiered pricing tables.** Instead of a flat $/sq-ft rate, a sign shop could publish rates segmented by ADT (Average Daily Traffic) of the install site — a known, public number (DOT data). High-traffic install = premium price, regardless of substrate cost.

- **Subscription/maintenance retainers.** Ongoing value capture via annual update contracts, illumination maintenance, seasonal changes. Converts a one-shot fabrication into a recurring attention-asset management relationship.

- **Outcome-anchored estimates.** For sophisticated clients, present the sign as a media buy: "This channel sign delivers X impressions/day at your effective CPM of $Y — comparable to digital display at $Z CPM, but with permanence." Reframes the buyer's reference price upward.

### The core structural insight

Sign companies are in the **attention business** but they price like **manufacturing businesses**. This is not irrational — it reflects genuine constraints (attribution opacity, one-shot transactions, buyer commoditization). But it is a strategic trap: manufacturing pricing commoditizes the product, suppresses margins, and prevents premium positioning.

The escape is not simply "charge more for value" (which is vague). It is: **adopt the attention industry's pricing primitive** (impressions, CPM, reach × frequency) as the anchor for conversations with buyers, and then price fabrication as the delivery mechanism for that attention asset.

## Corpus Answer

Industry pricing guides (Sign Builder Illustrated, shopVOX, Signs101 forum, SignCraft) universally prescribe cost-plus:

> **Materials cost + Labor (at $60–70/hr burden rate) + Overhead + Profit margin = Price**

Some sources acknowledge "value-based pricing" in the generic sense (charge for expertise, not just time), and premium shops add a design-value line item. The shift away from pure cost-plus is discussed in trade press but adoption is minimal.

The corpus does not:
- Frame signs as attention assets
- Reference CPM or impressions as a pricing anchor
- Suggest traffic-tiered pricing tables
- Draw analogy to media/advertising buy pricing

## Commentary

This spike illustrates the core failure mode of pricing by seller cost rather than buyer value: it forfeits the entire wedge between fabrication cost and attention value. That wedge is large for high-traffic locations and small for interior installs — which means the current flat pricing model systematically undercharges on the installs that matter most.

The novel framing here — sign shops as attention businesses priced as manufacturers — is a durable strategic lens, not just a tactical pricing adjustment.
