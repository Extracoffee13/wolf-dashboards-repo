# First-Principles Spike — 2026-11-19

## Question
When should a small sign shop print in-house, and when should it send the work to a wholesale (trade-only) printer?

(The backlog was missing, so I wrote this question myself. It bears directly on Brand 9, which already outsources to Signs365, WS Display and Gemini while keeping CNC, HDU and vinyl cutting in-house.)

## First-principles answer (written before any search)

**1. Break the cost into its parts.** Printing a job in-house costs:
- a fixed part F per period: the printer's capital spread over its life, floor space, the maintenance contract, software, and the operator's time whether or not the printer is busy;
- a variable part v per sq ft: ink, media, waste and the labour on that job.

So the unit cost at volume Q is v + F/Q. A trade printer's price p already includes its own v, its own F spread over very high volume, and its margin. You also pay shipping s and a coordination cost c: placing the order, proofing, and the risk that the order goes wrong.

**2. The crossover.** In-house wins when v + F/Q < p + s + c, which means Q > F / (p + s + c − v). The trade printer combines demand from hundreds of shops, so its printers run close to full time and it buys media by the truckload. Its unit cost is lower than yours unless your own printer is nearly as busy as theirs. For a shop with low or uneven volume, the break-even volume is high.

**3. The fixed cost is not just the machine. It is the owner's attention.** In a one- to three-person shop the scarce resource is the owner's hours, not the capital. Running a printer means profiles, head cleans, clogs, media changes and redoing jobs. Price those hours at what the owner earns selling or designing, and F roughly doubles or triples, so Q* goes up just as much. Most shops leave this out of their break-even math.

**4. Uncertainty favours a variable cost.** Outsourcing turns a fixed cost into a variable one. When demand is lumpy, as with builder rollouts, seasonal real estate work and one-off big jobs, a fixed cost is a bet on future volume. The variable cost keeps an option open: you pay only when there is a job. That option is worth most exactly when volume is least predictable.

**5. Speed and control are the real arguments for owning.** In-house printing gives same-day turnaround, same-day reprints and tight colour control. Their value is roughly (share of jobs that are rushed or likely to need a reprint) × (the premium those jobs earn, or the loss when one fails). If rush work is rare, this is a small number.

**6. Range of products.** A trade printer offers dozens of substrates and finishes. A shop can only make what its machines can make. Outsourcing widens the catalogue at no fixed cost.

**7. Blind shipping cuts the link between the brand and the equipment.** If the trade printer ships blind with the shop's return address, the customer cannot tell who printed the job. The shop's brand then lives in design, service and installation, not in the printer. This weakens the instinct that a "real" sign shop has to print its own work.

**8. Owning capacity changes how the shop sells.** Once a printer is bought, its sunk cost pushes the shop to steer sales toward what that printer can make, instead of what the market wants.

**9. Decide per product line, not for the whole shop.** Split the work along two axes:
- commodity, high volume and price-compared (coroplast yard signs, banners, standard A-frame faces) versus differentiated (CNC, HDU, dimensional letters, finishing, installation);
- predictable versus lumpy demand.

Outsource commodity print, where the trade printer's scale beats you. Own the differentiated, high-touch steps, where the margin and the moat are. Bring a commodity line in-house only when its steady volume clears Q* with the owner's time counted. Then check again whenever utilisation changes.

## Corpus answer (searched afterwards)
The trade press and practitioner forums agree on these points:
- **Volume drives the decision.** Compare monthly outsourced spend with in-house cost per sq ft. One signs101 example needs about 2,000 sq ft a month of 13 oz vinyl ($0.40/sq ft in-house against $1/sq ft outsourced). Signmedia.ca's analysis of a lease puts break-even at about 58–172 sq ft per day on one shift.
- **Capital and floor space** count against owning, and the trade press warns: "don't buy a printer and then go looking for the business to fill it."
- **In-house wins on control and same-day fixes.**
- **Hybrid model:** a common pattern is about 80% of the work in-house, with vetted wholesale partners taking overflow, oversize and specialty jobs. Vet the partner before you need one.
- **Trade printers** must stay out of retail sales so that shops can trust them.

## Delta category: **rediscovered**
The core answer matches the corpus: break-even on volume, outsourcing to avoid capital and floor space, in-house for control and speed, and a hybrid split. I reached all of it independently, including the warning that owning capacity bends sales (my point 8).

## Commentary
The core is rediscovered, but there are three framing differences the corpus does not state:
1. **The owner's attention as the main fixed cost.** The corpus prices labour per job but does not price the owner's opportunity cost in the break-even. For very small shops this moves Q* a lot.
2. **The direction of the hybrid is reversed.** The corpus starts from in-house and treats outsourcing as overflow. First principles start from outsourcing commodity print and own only the differentiated steps. For a design-led shop like Brand 9, with CNC, HDU and installation in-house and flat print sent to Signs365 and WS Display, the reversed version is the better fit, and it is what B9 already does.
3. **Option value and blind shipping.** The corpus does not frame outsourcing as an option worth most under lumpy demand. It also does not note that blind shipping makes the equipment invisible to the customer.

These are refinements, not corrections. The corpus is not wrong. It is written from the point of view of a print-centric shop.

**Takeaway for Brand 9:** do not buy a flatbed or roll printer until one commodity line has steady monthly volume above Q* with Bobby's hours priced in. Until then, the trade-printer route is the right default.

Confidence: 0.7. The core is well grounded. The size of the owner-attention effect is reasoned, not measured.
