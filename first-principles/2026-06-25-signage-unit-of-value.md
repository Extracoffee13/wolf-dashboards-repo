# Spike: What Is the Correct Unit of Value in a Commercial Signage Transaction?

**Date:** 2026-06-25  
**Question:** What is the correct unit of value in a commercial signage transaction — the physical artifact, the attention it captures, or the downstream decision it influences?  
**Category:** novel  
**Confidence:** 0.78

---

## Step 1 — The Question

Sign shops price by square footage. But the customer isn't buying aluminum substrate and vinyl laminate — they're buying something that happens in the world when people see the sign. What is that thing, exactly, and does the pricing model reflect it?

---

## Step 2 — First-Principles Derivation

### Define the object

A commercial sign is a **persistent, location-anchored communication device**. Two properties distinguish it from other advertising:

1. **Persistent**: always-on, 24/7, no recurring media buy. The cost is a one-time capital expenditure.  
2. **Location-anchored**: it exists at a fixed point in physical space. Its economic utility is a product of the message quality and the geography it occupies.

### What is the buyer actually purchasing?

The buyer (property owner, retailer, developer) does not want vinyl and aluminum. They want a specific event to occur repeatedly: a passerby notices, understands, and acts. The sign is a mechanism — not the goal.

Decomposing buyer goals:
- **Awareness** (notice): audience sees it exists  
- **Comprehension** (understand): audience reads and decodes the message  
- **Action** (decide): audience enters, calls, turns, buys, leases

The terminal unit of value is the **downstream decision influenced**. The intermediate unit (more measurable) is **attention captured** — impressions.

### Where does value reside?

- **In the artifact itself?** Partially. Quality fabrication affects durability, visual sharpness, and nighttime legibility. But a flawlessly fabricated sign in a zero-traffic alley has near-zero economic value to the buyer.
- **In the location?** Dominantly. A mediocre banner on a 50,000-cars/day arterial delivers more advertiser value than a premium illuminated monument in an empty lot. Location drives impression volume; impression volume is the raw material of sign value.
- **In the downstream decision?** This is the terminal value — the lead generated, the lease signed, the customer who walked in. But it is unattributable without complex infrastructure (call tracking, footfall sensors, conversion analytics).

### Why does the market price on square footage?

The fabricator's cost function is approximately linear with area: materials (vinyl, aluminum, acrylic) are priced per square foot; labor time scales with area for most sign types. Square footage is:

- Objectively measurable and verifiable by both parties  
- A reasonable proxy for fabrication cost  
- Standardized enough to enable competitive bidding  

But it is **zero correlated** with the sign's location, audience size, or downstream impact. A $2,000 sign (10 sq ft × $200/sq ft) on a 60,000-impression-per-day highway and the same $2,000 sign on a suburban side street cost the buyer the same — despite a potential 100:1 difference in impression delivery.

### The structural comparison: fabricators vs. OOH companies

The outdoor advertising industry (Lamar, Clear Channel, OUTFRONT) solved this problem. Their pricing unit is **CPM (cost per thousand impressions)**, derived from:

- Traffic counts (AADT from DOT data)  
- Demographic profiles (audience composition)  
- Visibility ratings (DEC — Daily Effective Circulation, which discounts for viewing angle, speed, obstructions)  

How did they get here? **Vertical integration into location ownership.** By owning or long-leasing the right-of-way, billboard companies own the input that makes impressions possible. They don't sell structures — they sell eyeballs. The structure is a cost item on their P&L, not the product.

### The fabricator's trap

A sign fabricator like Brand 9 Signs sells artifacts but owns no locations. Therefore:

1. They cannot guarantee impressions — they don't control the traffic that makes impressions happen.  
2. They cannot price on CPM — the input to that calculation (location) belongs to the buyer.  
3. They are structurally limited to cost-plus pricing because that is the only dimension fully under their control.

This creates a **systematic mispricing**:
- High-traffic placement: fabricator captures ~0% of the location premium; buyer captures all the upside.  
- Low-traffic placement: buyer overpays (relative to value received) but often doesn't know it.

### What would escape the trap look like?

Four structural moves are available:

**A. Own/control locations** → Become an OOH media company. Price on CPM. Massive capital requirement; different business model entirely.

**B. Bundle location intelligence** → Advise clients on optimal placement, size-for-speed (legibility at vehicle speed), setback calculations, sight-line analysis. Price this advisory layer as a premium service above fabrication. Capture part of the location premium without owning the location.

**C. Certify outcomes** → Deploy traffic-count APIs, camera-based impression analytics, or mobile signal aggregation to deliver impression guarantees. Price a portion of the sign on performance. Creates durable competitive differentiation.

**D. Move upmarket to experiential/identity work** → Custom wayfinding systems, branded environments, architectural signage. At this level, design quality, brand alignment, and spatial experience become the differentiator — not area. Value-based pricing is more feasible because the output is less commoditized.

### First-principles conclusion

- The **terminal unit of value** in a signage transaction is the downstream decision influenced by the sign.  
- The **practical intermediate unit** is attention captured (impressions).  
- The **fabrication pricing unit** (sq ft) is a cost proxy, not a value proxy.  
- The **gap** between cost-proxy and value pricing is the spread the OOH industry has captured by owning locations.  
- Fabricators without locations can partially close this gap via advisory services, outcome guarantees, or moving upmarket — but cannot fully close it without controlling the location variable.

**Durable rule:** Any business that prices on cost inputs rather than value outputs will systematically undercharge for high-value placements and overcharge for low-value ones — losing margin in the former and deals in the latter.

## Step 3 — Corpus Answer

The standard sign industry literature confirms:

- **Square footage is the dominant pricing unit** for fabricators: $15–$50/sq ft for most substrate types; $150–$400/sq ft for monuments and freestanding structures.  
- **Materials, labor, overhead, and markup** are the cost components. Typical gross margin targets are 40–60%.  
- **Value-based considerations** are mentioned ("your location, the cost of the sign, and where your customers are coming from") but are not operationalized into a pricing mechanism. They appear as advisory context, not as a pricing input.  
- **CPM for outdoor signage** IS discussed in the corpus — one source notes that a typical business sign costs less than $0.30 per thousand impressions, far below TV ($10–$56 CPM). This is framed as a **sales argument to buyers** (signs are cost-effective vs. other media), not as a **pricing reform argument to fabricators**.  
- The OOH industry's CPM/DEC model is described separately from sign fabricator pricing, with no bridge connecting the two.

## Commentary

For Brand 9 Signs specifically: the business is currently competing on fabrication quality and turnaround in a cost-plus market. The highest-leverage strategic move is likely Option B (location intelligence advisory) — it requires no capital, leverages existing relationships with developers and property owners, and creates a reason for the client to share project details (location, traffic, intended audience) that enable better design decisions anyway.

The wayfinding market (interior signage systems, campus navigation, retail environments) is where Option D plays out: designers command value-based fees because the output is spatial problem-solving, not material area.
