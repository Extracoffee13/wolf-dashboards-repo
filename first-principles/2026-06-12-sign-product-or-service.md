# Spike: Is a commercial sign a product or a service?

**Date:** 2026-06-12
**Category:** novel
**Confidence:** 0.75

---

## The Question

When Brand 9 Signs sells and installs a channel-letter building sign, what has it delivered — a product or a service? The answer determines pricing strategy, competitive positioning, customer relationship structure, and where the real margin lives.

---

## Step 2 — First-Principles Derivation

**Starting primitives:**

A **product** is an artifact that (a) is manufactured, (b) exists independently of its maker post-sale, (c) transfers ownership cleanly, and (d) retains value proportional to its physical condition.

A **service** is a process performed for a buyer whose value (a) is inseparable from delivery context, (b) decays or vanishes if context changes, and (c) is priced on value delivered, not materials consumed.

**Decomposing what a sign actually is:**

A commercial sign has three irreducible components:

1. **The Message** — design, layout, copy, brand encoding. This is knowledge work — it requires expertise, iteration, relationship with the client. Service-like.
2. **The Substrate** — metal, vinyl, LED modules, acrylic. This is manufactured material. Product-like.
3. **The Placement value** — the spatial relationship between the sign and its audience (foot traffic, vehicle speed, sightlines, zoning). This is NOT owned by the buyer or seller — it's a function of location and context. Neither product nor service — it's *ambient infrastructure*.

**The ownership-transfer test:**

If it were a pure product, a sign should have residual resale value. Does it?

A "Smith's Auto Repair" channel letter sign has near-zero resale value to anyone except Smith. Compare to a CNC machine, which retains value to any manufacturer. Signs are highly specific — they encode one entity's identity and are spatially calibrated to one location. Only the substrate has commodity resale value (scrap metal, generic LED modules). The message and placement value are irrecoverable.

Conclusion: the dominant economic value — the message — does NOT transfer in a product sense.

**The value-decay test:**

Products depreciate with physical wear. Services are consumed at delivery. How does a sign's value depreciate?

- A 5-year-old sign for an active business at a busy corner may retain 100% of its communication value.
- A 1-month-old sign for a closed business has 0% communication value despite near-perfect physical condition.

Value is driven by `message relevance × audience flow`, not physical state. This is a service characteristic: value is contextual and relational, not inherent in the artifact.

**The substitution test:**

Can you swap a sign from one supplier for an identical one from another, and preserve full value? 

- The substrate: increasingly yes (vinyl, aluminum, LEDs are commoditized).
- The design encoding: no — it encodes brand identity built through relationship and expertise.
- The installation calibration (height, angle, lighting balance for a specific location): no — this requires local knowledge.

The differentiated, value-driving portions are non-substitutable — a service characteristic.

**The pricing implication:**

Under product logic: price = (materials + labor) × markup. Customers shop price. Margin erodes to commodity levels. The sign company competes on fabrication efficiency.

Under service logic: price = value of communication delivered. A sign generating $200K/year in new customer attention can price at $15–20K even with $2K material cost. The 7–10x markup is justified by expertise and outcome, not inputs. Customers buy on confidence and relationship.

**First-principles conclusion:**

A commercial sign is an **information service delivered via a durable physical artifact**. The substrate is the delivery vehicle. The economic value is the communication function: attention capture, brand reinforcement, wayfinding — all context-dependent, relationship-intensive, and measured in outcomes rather than inputs.

The sign company's correct operating model is therefore a **service firm that happens to manufacture delivery artifacts** — not a manufacturing shop that happens to offer design. Pricing, positioning, sales process, and competitive moat should all follow service-firm logic.

## Step 3 — Corpus Answer

**Industry practice (signage trades):**

The signage industry overwhelmingly classifies itself as manufacturing/fabrication. Pricing guides center on: materials list + labor hours + overhead + margin. The standard advice is cost-plus pricing calibrated against local market rates. Signs101 (the industry's largest practitioner forum) frames all pricing discussions around material and labor cost recovery.

**Accounting/tax classification:**

Permanent signage is capitalized as a fixed asset and depreciated (typically 7–15 years). Temporary signage is expensed in the year incurred. Tax authorities treat signs as property fixtures — firmly product-category.

**Marketing theory (IHIP framework):**

Academic service marketing (Zeithaml, Parasuraman & Berry) uses signage as an example of a *tangible cue* that helps service businesses overcome intangibility. Signs are how service companies make themselves credible and visible. In this framing, signs are a *tool for service delivery* — but the SIGN COMPANY ITSELF is still implicitly treated as a product manufacturer.

**Service-dominant logic (Vargo & Lusch, 2004):**

S-D Logic argues all economic exchange is fundamentally about service — goods are just "appliances" for service delivery. Under SDL, a sign is a "service vehicle." But this framework hasn't penetrated signage industry practice.

**Corpus summary:** The industry self-identifies as product/manufacturing. Academic marketing uses signs as instruments of service delivery but doesn't apply service-firm logic to the sign company itself.

## Step 4 — Delta

**Category: NOVEL**

The corpus contains two framings:
1. Sign company = manufacturer (industry default)
2. Signs = tangible cues for service businesses (marketing theory)

Neither framing asks: *what if the sign company reconceived itself as a service firm?*

My first-principles derivation arrives at a third framing: **the sign company is a service firm whose service is "delivered communication" and whose artifact is the delivery mechanism**. This inverts the industry default and has direct strategic consequences:

- Move from cost-plus to value-based pricing
- Compete on expertise and outcomes, not fabrication speed
- Build recurring revenue (maintenance, refreshes, expansions) as natural service recurrence
- Sales conversation shifts from "what do you want built?" to "what does your audience need to understand about you?"

The corpus doesn't contain this synthesis applied to the sign industry's own positioning. It's available by combining S-D Logic with signage industry analysis, but no practitioner or academic source does this explicitly.

## Commentary

This matters for Brand 9 Signs operationally. If you price as a manufacturer, you'll be compared to online vinyl printers and race to the bottom. If you price as a communication design firm that delivers physical artifacts, you're competing with brand agencies — and winning on tangibility and local expertise. The question "product or service?" is actually the question "who are your competitors?"

The practical move: restructure the sales discovery process around communication objectives (who is the audience, what must they understand, what action should they take) before ever discussing materials or format. That's how service firms sell.
